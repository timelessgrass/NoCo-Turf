A zap off artificial turf is the doorknob shock you get after crossing a carpet, with a plastic lawn in place of the carpet. It startles more than it hurts. Here is where the charge comes from, and which fixes outlast a few minutes.

## Why does artificial turf give me static shocks?

Because walking on it charges you, and nothing in the lawn takes the charge away.

- **Contact makes the charge.** When two materials touch and pull apart, electrons move from one surface to the other. The EOS/ESD Association, the standards body for static control in electronics, says how much charge builds depends on the contact area, how fast the surfaces separate, the relative humidity and the materials ([ESD Association](https://www.esda.org/esd-overview/esd-fundamentals/part-1-an-introduction-to-esd/)). Each step presses a sole into plastic blades and lifts it off again.
- **Plastic holds it.** Turf yarn is nylon, polyethylene, polypropylene or polyester. The ESD Association notes that a considerable charge can build on an insulator and stay in place for a very long time. A U.S. patent on antistatic turf says synthetic turf can build up charge "in itself as well as the persons who walk or play on it" ([U.S. patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10323361B1/en)).
- **Metal lets it go.** A grounded conductor drains charge easily, so the shock lands at the first metal or person you touch after crossing the lawn: the gate latch, a fence post, the grill lid, a friend's hand.

There is a quieter sign before any spark. Royal Grass, a European turf maker, mentions clothes clinging to the blades when you lie on the lawn ([Royal Grass](https://royalgrass.com/blogs/why-is-my-artificial-turf-static/)).

## Why does dry Northern Colorado air make it worse?

Dry air lets charge pile up on most surfaces. The ESD Association's table of typical static voltages shows how much humidity matters:

| Activity | At 10–25% relative humidity | At 65–90% relative humidity |
|---|---|---|
| Walking across carpet | 35,000 volts | 1,500 volts |
| Walking across vinyl tile | 12,000 volts | 250 volts |

On most afternoons, Northern Colorado's humidity sits in that left-hand column or below it. The Colorado Climate Center lists low relative humidity among the characteristic features of the eastern plains' climate ([Colorado Climate Center](https://climate.colostate.edu/climate_long.html)). CoAgMET, the Climate Center's network of research weather stations, recorded this in 2025:

| CoAgMET station, 2025 | Average daily low humidity | Average daily high humidity | Days the low fell to 25% or less | Days it fell to 15% or less |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Fort Collins](/areas/fort-collins-co/), CSU campus | 28.4% | 82.7% | 200 | 83 |
| [Greeley](/areas/greeley-co/), Greeley 4 | 27.6% | 87.8% | 221 | 107 |

Station data: [Fort Collins](https://coagmet.colostate.edu/data/daily/fcl01.json?from=2025-01-01&to=2025-12-31&fields=rhMax,rhMaxTime,rhMin,rhMinTime), [Greeley 4](https://coagmet.colostate.edu/data/daily/gly04.json?from=2025-01-01&to=2025-12-31&fields=rhMax,rhMaxTime,rhMin,rhMinTime).

What those numbers mean for static:

- **It peaks in the afternoon.** The day's low humidity came between late morning and early evening on 88% of days in Fort Collins and 91% in Greeley; the high came overnight or near dawn. A yard that starts the day in the humid column of the voltage table ends it in the dry one, so a dewy morning is the least likely time for a zap.
- **It isn't only a winter problem.** Every month of 2025 had afternoons at 25% humidity or less at both stations, and March had the most.
- **Winter brings dry wind.** The Climate Center says winter on the plains "more often" brings dry air and strong winds, and the National Weather Service defines the chinook as a warm, dry wind in the lee of the Rockies, fairly common in winter ([National Weather Service](https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=chinook)).

One caution before blaming the weather for everything. A white paper by one of that patent's inventors says humidity "does not seem to play as important a role in synthetic turf" as it does in carpet, and that static builds in low and high humidity alike ([Static Dissipative Turf](https://web.archive.org/web/20251118135150/https://www.foreverlawn.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/WhitePaper-Static-Dissipative-Turf-rev-IA-before-EA-2.pdf)). Dry air helps; it is not the whole cause.

## Why do kids get zapped coming off a plastic slide?

Because the slide makes a charge and the turf won't drain it. GameTime, a playground maker, says static builds along the surface as a child goes down a plastic slide, and that how much depends on the weather and the clothes ([GameTime](https://www.gametime.com/news/designing-playgrounds-for-children-with-cochlear-implants)). The antistatic-turf patent calls playgrounds a particular problem: much of the equipment is an insulator, turf can act as one too, and charge from a slide isn't dissipated as the child walks across the turf "until a conductive ground, such as a metal pole, is touched."

What helps on a play area:

- **A metal slide.** In GameTime's research, stainless steel was the one slide material with zero discharge. The manufacturing changes and coatings it tried on plastic slides worked at first, and none lasted.
- **Care around cochlear implants.** Advanced Bionics, a cochlear implant maker, lists sliding on plastic slides among common causes of static discharge. It advises removing the sound processor and headpiece before playing on plastic slides, plastic tubes and trampolines, or steering the child to other equipment ([Advanced Bionics](https://www.advancedbionics.com/content/dam/advancedbionics/documents/libraries/tools-for-schools/ToolsforSchools-Static-Electricity-and-Cochlear-Implants-Information-Flyer.pdf.coredownload.pdf)). The child's audiologist has the final word.
- **Placement and anchoring** of the set are their own subject: [swing sets on artificial turf](/guides/swing-set-on-artificial-turf/) and our [playground turf](/services/playground-turf/) page. What else can go under a set is compared in [playground turf vs mulch](/guides/playground-turf-vs-mulch/).

## How do I stop the shocks right now?

Nothing in this section is permanent. Here is what each fix buys:

| Fix | Why it helps | How long it lasts |
|---|---|---|
| A light mist from the hose | Royal Grass says it helps discharge the static | Until the blades dry, which is fast here |
| Leather-soled shoes, cotton clothes | Less charge builds on you | While you wear them |
| An antistatic spray | Coats the blades | Until rain or foot traffic removes it |
| Touching grounded metal on purpose | Drains your charge where you choose | One shock you expected, then it builds again |

**Mist before play, not all afternoon.** Royal Grass says moistening statically charged turf is often enough. In Northern Colorado the effect is short: the Colorado Climate Center notes that low humidity "favors rapid evaporation," and static is worst in exactly the driest hours. The same quick drying limits a rinse for heat; see [how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/).

Watering hours matter too. Windsor's standing rule is no lawn watering from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. ([Town of Windsor](https://www.windsorco.gov/1222/Efficiency)), and Fort Collins' voluntary 2026 Water Shortage Watch asks residents not to water in the same hours ([City of Fort Collins](https://www.fortcollins.gov/News-Articles/News/2026-Q2/Voluntary-Water-Shortage-Watch)). Whether a hand mist on turf counts as watering depends on how each rule is written; [do you have to water artificial turf](/guides/do-you-have-to-water-artificial-turf/) goes through them. It depends on your address, so ask your water provider. Not legal advice.

**Change what's on your feet.** Advanced Bionics' static advice for families applies to anyone: leather soles instead of rubber, and natural fibers such as cotton. The patent's test walker, for comparison, wore rubber-soled sneakers.

**Go carefully with sprays.** Royal Grass notes antistatic products sold at DIY stores. The patent says chemicals sprayed on turf work in some situations but "are not substantive solutions for outdoor environments, especially in the presence of rain," and the white paper says they wash off with rain and wear off under foot traffic. Whatever goes on the blades ends up in the yard. Read the label and ask the turf's maker.

## What should I ask for if static matters, as under a play set?

Ask before the turf goes in, because lasting static control is part of the product, not something added later.

- **There is no yardstick.** The white paper says "there are no accepted standards for static control in synthetic turf," and that carpet test methods have proved ineffective for turf.
- **The approaches aren't equal.** The same paper rates a topical treatment moderately effective but not permanent, an antistatic additive in the yarn permanent but ineffective, and an antistatic filament tufted in with the blades very effective and permanent. The patent describes that filament: a carbon core in a non-conductive sheath, in as few as 1 of every 32 tuft rows. Both documents come from the design's inventors and the maker that sells it, not from independent tests.
- **New turf proves little.** In the patent's own outdoor test, a person walked each sample for 30 seconds and then touched a grounded metal pole. Ordinary turf gave no shock when new or after a rinse, and gave one after 2 months outdoors.

Questions for an installer:

- Does this product have static-dissipative yarn? Is it an antistatic filament tufted in with the blades, an additive in the yarn, or a coating?
- What test shows it still works after weathering, not only on new turf?
- Is it written on the spec sheet? Our [turf spec sheet guide](/guides/turf-spec-sheet-for-colorado/) covers what else that sheet should say.
- For a play area: which equipment is plastic, and where is the nearest metal a child will touch?

A good answer names the method, points to a test on weathered turf, and says plainly that there is no industry standard to cite.
