answer:
  question: "Is artificial turf or xeriscape (plants and mulch) the better lawn replacement for my yard?"
  answer: "Usually both, in different places. Turf suits the parts kids and dogs use hard. Planted xeriscape suits front yards and sunny borders: plants cool the ground, feed pollinators and can earn plant rebates. A rock-only yard stores heat and still gets weeds. Wellington, Firestone and Frederick set front-yard live-plant minimums that neither turf nor rock counts toward."
faq:
  - q: "Is xeriscape just rock?"
    a: "No. CSU Extension describes xeriscape as planning, improving the soil, choosing suitable plants, and watering, mulching and maintaining well, and it can include water-conserving grasses such as buffalograss or blue grama. Rock is one of the mulches a xeriscape can use, not the design itself."
  - q: "Does artificial turf count as xeriscape?"
    a: "Not in Fort Collins, whose city code defines xeriscape landscaping so that it excludes artificial turf. How other towns and HOAs treat it is in our guide to whether artificial turf counts as xeriscape."
  - q: "Can wood chips replace rock between the plants?"
    a: "They can, with upkeep. CSU Extension says wood chips in Colorado's intense sun can turn water-repellent, so it doesn't recommend them in xeriscapes unless they are checked and spread out often, and persistent wind can blow them away. Under trees or in watered beds they hold up better."
  - q: "What size of gravel works as mulch, and how deep?"
    a: "Stones under about half an inch across, according to CSU Extension; larger rock does little for water or weeds and can hold plants back. CSU's PlantTalk suggests about 3 inches for weed control, and CSU's mulching guide says four."
  - q: "Can the planted part of a mixed yard still earn a rebate?"
    a: "Possibly, if it meets the program's plant rules. Fort Collins Utilities' Xeriscape Incentive Program requires 50% plant coverage at maturity in the renovated area, and Windsor's front-yard lawn-replacement rebate requires living plants on 50% of the project and excludes artificial turf. Our rebates guide has each provider's current terms and dates."

**Checked September 25, 2026.** Many Coloradans say "xeriscape" and picture a yard of rock, so this page compares three lawn replacements: artificial turf, rock on its own, and planted xeriscape, meaning water-wise plants with gravel or wood mulch between them. Whether turf counts as xeriscape under a town code or HOA is answered in [is artificial turf considered xeriscape](/guides/is-artificial-turf-considered-xeriscape/).

| | Artificial turf | Rock on its own | Planted xeriscape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kids and dogs | Takes daily play and paws | Fine for a path, poor for play | Needs paths; plants don't take trampling |
| Full summer sun | Far hotter than grass at the surface | Stores heat and gives it off toward the house | Plants shade the soil and cool it |
| Weeds | Airborne seed in the infill, roots creeping in at the edges | Weeds root through fabric laid under the stones | Heavy weeding until plants cover the soil |
| Snow | Leave it, or clear with plastic tools | Shovels and snowblowers pick up stones | Welcome water; heavy piles bend small shrubs |
| Water once it's in | No irrigation | None | Regular water to establish, then little |
| Front-yard live-plant rules | Doesn't count | Doesn't count | Counts |

## Which parts of the yard suit turf, rock or planted beds?

- **Where kids play and dogs run: turf.** CSU Extension keeps children's play areas and pet exercise areas in turfgrass "that can take heavy wear" ([CSU Fact Sheet 7.234](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/xeriscaping-retrofit-your-yard/)). CSU means living grass; a planted bed can't take that wear, and artificial turf can. Turf or living grass there: see [artificial turf vs sod](/guides/artificial-turf-vs-sod/) and [low-water grass instead of turf](/guides/artificial-turf-vs-low-water-grass/).
- **Front yards and sunny borders: planted xeriscape.** The EPA notes that vegetation feeds and shelters pollinating insects ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/heatislands/benefits-trees-and-vegetation)), and CSU says a pollinator planting needs species blooming from early spring into late fall ([CSU Fact Sheet 5.616](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/creating-pollinator-habitat/)).
- **Narrow strips:** CSU says grass strips narrower than 8 feet are hard to water with spray heads and suggests xeric plantings or hardscape. Turf needs no spray heads, so a strip can go either way; see [artificial turf in a side yard](/guides/artificial-turf-side-yard/).

## Which one stays coolest in a Northern Colorado summer?

Planted beds. Turf and bare rock both run hot, in different ways: turf heats up at the surface, while rock stores heat and passes it to the soil and nearby walls. The Fort Collins station averages 29.5 days a year at or above 90°F and Greeley UNC 58.3 (NOAA 1991–2020 normals).

- **Turf.** Penn State's sports-surface researchers put synthetic turf 35 to 55°F above natural grass on a hot day ([how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/)), and the Synthetic Turf Council reports occasional cases of fibers melting from sun reflected off Low-E windows ([turf melting near windows](/guides/artificial-turf-melting-from-windows/)).
- **Rock.** CSU's PlantTalk says stone mulches "store and radiate heat" and warns against large areas of unshaded rock next to a house ([PlantTalk 1905](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/water-wise-xeriscape/1905-xeriscape-mulches/)). CSU's mulching guide adds that gravel passes more heat into the soil than wood chips and can pass it on to buildings ([CSU Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/mulching/)).
- **Planted beds.** The EPA says vegetation lowers surface and air temperatures by shading the ground and evaporating water through its leaves. The mulch between plants matters less: CSU says gravel-mulched beds stay cooler on the surface than paving, and sunny wood mulch can run hotter than gravel.

## Which one keeps weeds down?

None is weed-free; they differ in where weeds start and whether that improves.

- **Rock on its own.** Fabric underneath doesn't fix it: CSU says weeds germinate on top of woven fabric and root into or through it ([GardenNotes #245](https://cmg.extension.colostate.edu/Gardennotes/245.pdf)), and that weed fabric doesn't prevent weeds in permanent plantings. Rock larger than about half an inch across does little against weeds.
- **Planted xeriscape.** More weeding at first, less later. PlantTalk says mulch holds weeds down until maturing plants cover the soil and leave weeds little light ([PlantTalk 1910](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/water-wise-xeriscape/1910-xeriscape-maintenance/)). A rock-only yard never reaches that shaded stage.
- **Turf.** FieldTurf Landscape, a turf maker, says weeds can't grow up through the backing but airborne seed can sprout in the infill, mostly near the edges. TigerTurf, another maker, says roots from neighboring beds can creep under the edging and up through the backing, which matters wherever turf meets planting. See [why weeds grow through artificial turf](/guides/weeds-growing-through-artificial-turf/).

## What happens to each one under snow and de-icer?

The Loveland 2N station averages 47.0 inches of snow a year, and Fort Collins and Windsor tell residents to shovel sidewalk snow into the yard, not the street. Whatever lines the walk gets the pile.

- **Planted beds** welcome it. PlantTalk calls heavy Front Range snow welcome moisture that cuts the need for winter watering, though small shrubs flattened by snow piles may need pruning ([PlantTalk 1754](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/trees-shrubs-vines/1754-snow-impacts-woody-herbaceous-plants/)).
- **De-icer is the planted beds' weak spot.** CSU says granular magnesium chloride is commonly spread on sidewalks and driveways, that along roads it moves into the soil with precipitation, and that woody plants taking it up burn at the leaf edges and needle tips ([CSU Fact Sheet 7.425](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/magnesium-chloride-toxicity-in-trees/)). FieldTurf says magnesium chloride won't harm turf, while regular salt can scuff the fibers.
- **Rock** is hard to clear: Toro's single-stage snowblower manual says the machine isn't designed for gravel and can throw stones.
- **Turf** can wait for the melt or be cleared with plastic tools: see [snow removal on artificial turf](/guides/artificial-turf-snow-removal/).

## How do they handle water on clay, and how much do new plants need?

- **Planted beds** keep needing water, most of it early. CSU says even drought-tolerant plants need regular, relatively heavy watering until they establish, from days to years depending on plant size, and new flowers may need water daily for two weeks ([CSU Fact Sheet 7.240](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/watering-a-home-landscape-during-drought/)). PlantTalk says watering can drop after one or two years. Some favorite perennials, CSU warns, use as much water as a lawn or more.
- **Timing.** CSU says to weigh water availability before converting; with Johnstown on a 2026 Level 3 schedule of 2 assigned days a week, ask your provider about new plantings first.
- **Beds near the house.** CSU notes that Colorado clay can stay waterlogged under a dry-looking surface, and advises keeping sprinkler spray at least five feet from the foundation ([PlantTalk 1617](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/soils-amendments-composting/1617-expansive-soils/)). The Colorado Geological Survey lists planting against a foundation so irrigation water soaks in among the problems on expansive soil ([CGS](https://coloradogeologicalsurvey.org/hazards/expansive-soil-rock/)).
- **Rock** needs no water. CSU says pea gravel improves infiltration in thunderstorms, while larger rocks increase runoff.
- **Turf** needs no irrigation; its zones get capped or become drip for the beds ([sprinklers after turf](/guides/sprinkler-system-after-artificial-turf/)). On clay, the grade and base decide whether it drains: see [does artificial turf drain](/guides/does-artificial-turf-drain/) and [artificial turf on clay soil](/guides/artificial-turf-on-clay-soil/).

## Why can't a front yard be all turf or all rock in some towns?

Because several Northern Colorado codes require a share of living plants, and neither turf nor rock is living.

| Town | What the code requires | Where it applies |
|---|---|---|
| [Wellington](/areas/wellington-co/) | At least 75% live materials between the front of the house and the curb, driveway excluded | New single-family subdivision lots |
| [Firestone](/areas/firestone-co/) | Live plants on 75% of the front yard and 50% of the combined side and rear yards; artificial turf can't count | Single-family lots |
| [Frederick](/areas/frederick-co/) | At least 50% living plant coverage at maturity in every landscaped area; at most 50% nonliving, such as rock or wood mulch | New development and redevelopment; single-family yards must meet the parts that apply |
| Milliken | At least 50% live materials at maturity in front yards | New single- and two-family development |

Frederick's code also says water conservation does not include "large mulched (including gravel) beds" or "areas without landscape plant material," so a front yard of rock alone doesn't fit it. How Frederick treats a redone existing yard depends on the project: ask Frederick Planning. Whether a Firestone plan meets the live-plant share is the Town's call: ask Firestone Planning.

Colorado law requires every HOA to preapprove at least 3 water-wise garden designs for front yards, while its protection for artificial turf covers backyards only ([SB23-178](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_files/92307/download)). Not legal advice; see [HOA approval for artificial turf](/guides/hoa-turf-approval/). Other towns are in [artificial turf rules in Northern Colorado](/guides/turf-rules-northern-colorado/); plant rebates are in [turf rebates](/guides/turf-rebates-northern-colorado/).

## How do you combine turf, rock and plants in one yard?

1. **Turf** where people and dogs use the ground: the play area, the dog's route, a putting green.
2. **Planted beds** along the front, in sunny borders, and wherever a code or HOA wants living plants.
3. **Rock** as the mulch in those beds, in stones under about half an inch, and on paths.
4. **Water** only where plants grow: drip in the beds, kept back from the foundation. Trees that drank from the old lawn need a plan: see [watering trees after turf](/guides/watering-trees-with-artificial-turf/).

Proportions and layouts are in [mixing turf, rock and plant beds](/guides/mixing-turf-rock-and-plant-beds/); a dog run is in [pea gravel vs turf for a dog run](/guides/pea-gravel-vs-turf-dog-run/). Before you sign a plan from an installer or landscaper, ask:

- **Which areas count toward my town's live-plant share?** A good answer puts the percentage on the drawing.
- **Which sprinkler zones get capped, and which become drip?** A good answer names the zones.
- **How is the edge built where turf meets a bed?** A good answer names the edging and what keeps roots and mulch from crossing it; see [turf edging options](/guides/artificial-turf-edging-options/).

For the turf itself, see [artificial turf installation](/services/artificial-turf-installation/).
