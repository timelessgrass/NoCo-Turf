**Checked September 25, 2026.** Artificial turf takes one pollen source out of your yard: the lawn. Whether that helps depends on which pollen you react to and how much of it blows in from somewhere else. This page sorts the two, then takes up the rarer question of reacting to turf materials. It is general information, not medical advice; an allergist can tell you which pollen is yours.

## What does artificial turf take away for someone with grass allergies?

Three things, all inside your own fence:

- **Your lawn's pollen.** CSU Extension, writing for Colorado gardeners, says the basic assumption for a new lawn is that "Kentucky bluegrass must be planted" ([GardenNotes #561](https://cmg.extension.colostate.edu/Gardennotes/561.pdf)), and National Jewish Health names Kentucky bluegrass among the most common grass allergens, with grass pollen on its list of five common Colorado allergens ([National Jewish Health](https://www.nationaljewish.org/education/health-information/allergies/5-common-colorado-allergens-that-can-get-in-the-way-of-gardening)). CSU says bluegrass, perennial ryegrass and tall fescue all flower and set seedheads in spring, and that mowing lower "will not eliminate flowering" ([PlantTalk 1557](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/lawns/1557-turf-sending-many-seedheads/)). Turf blades are commonly polyethylene, polypropylene or nylon ([Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/schools/artificial-turf)). They don't flower.
- **The mowing.** National Jewish Health suggests wearing a mask to mow, or having someone else do it. Turf needs no mowing.
- **Touching grass.** The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology says grass can also cause hives or welts, much less often than nose and eye symptoms, and "usually, actual contact with grass causes them" ([AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies)).

National Jewish Health's own suggestion is to replace turf grass with groundcovers or other plants you aren't allergic to. Artificial turf is another way to take the grass out, though NJH doesn't mention it.

What turf can't do is clean the air. NJH says high winds "pull pollens into the air and carry them for miles." A turf yard in a neighborhood of bluegrass lawns still gets grass pollen on a windy June afternoon.

## What pollen still reaches a turf yard in Northern Colorado?

If grass isn't your main trigger, most of it.

| Source | When | Does turf change it? |
|---|---|---|
| Your own lawn | Late spring and summer (NJH) | Yes: nothing flowers, nothing to mow |
| Neighbors' lawns, parks, school fields | Same | No: the wind brings it |
| Cottonwood pollen | April (CSU Extension) | No |
| Cottonwood cotton | June | CSU says it isn't an allergen; it lands on the turf as debris |
| Ash, elm, maple, box elder, juniper | Spring; nationally, February through April (AAFA) | No |
| Ragweed, sagebrush, Russian thistle, burning bush | Late summer and fall (NJH, AAFA) | No, unless they grow in your yard |
| Mold | When you handle dried leaves, soil, compost or bark mulch (NJH) | Partly; see below |

*Sources: [National Jewish Health](https://www.nationaljewish.org/education/health-information/allergies/5-common-colorado-allergens-that-can-get-in-the-way-of-gardening), [CSU PlantTalk 1758](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/trees-shrubs-vines/1758-cottonwood-cotton/), [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pollen-allergy/).*

**Cottonwoods.** CSU Extension says male cottonwoods shed pollen in April, and that a few municipalities restrict male trees "because the April pollen causes problems for allergy sufferers." The June cotton "has no allergenic properties." National Jewish Health adds that a flare while the cotton flies is either irritation from the cotton or grass pollinating in summer. In a turf yard, that grass is someone else's. Clearing the cotton is in [leaves and cottonwood fluff on turf](/guides/leaves-on-artificial-turf/).

**Street trees.** In Fort Collins, a 2003 [USDA Forest Service analysis](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/download/48728.pdf) found green ash was the most abundant street tree, at 22% of them, with American elm, Siberian elm and silver maple among the other important species. Ash, elm and maple are all on AAFA's list of the trees that cause the most allergy symptoms. The count is old, and the ground under a tree doesn't change its pollen.

**Weeds.** NJH lists ragweed among Colorado's common allergens, pollinating mostly in late summer and fall. AAFA's weed list also names Russian thistle, burning bush, sagebrush and tumbleweed. Weeds that come up through turf edges or rock beds can flower like any other; see [weeds growing through artificial turf](/guides/weeds-growing-through-artificial-turf/).

AAFA's months are national, and it notes that where you live changes when you have symptoms.

## Does pollen build up on artificial turf?

Yes. SYNLawn's care manual says turf "will accumulate dust, pollen, dirt etc." and benefits from routine washing ([SYNLawn](https://www.synlawn.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/SYNLawn-Care-and-Maintenance-ENG.pdf)), and CSU Extension says turf collects "wind-blown dirt and debris that must somehow be removed" ([PlantTalk 1537](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/lawns/1537-artificial-turf-lawns/)). Rain won't do much of the rinsing here: the Colorado Climate Center describes the plains as having "infrequent rains and snow" and "moderate to high wind movement" ([Colorado Climate Center](https://climate.colostate.edu/climate_long.html)).

For an allergic household, that means:

- **Rinse it through the season,** most of all after windy days in grass and tree season. The method, and keeping the water out of the gutter, is in [how to clean artificial turf](/guides/how-to-clean-artificial-turf/).
- **Pick the time.** The American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology says pollen counts usually peak in midmorning and early evening, and advises staying in when the wind is blowing pollen around ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/)). If the allergic person does the rinsing, a calm hour outside those windows is the one to pick.
- **Keep the beds in mind.** NJH suggests rock mulch over bark mulch for mold allergies, and AAFA says insect-pollinated plants such as roses and cherry and pear trees "do not cause allergic rhinitis." Where the beds go is in [mixing turf, rock and plant beds](/guides/mixing-turf-rock-and-plant-beds/).

**Mold is the partial case.** Turf covers the soil, but leaves left on it in a shady, damp corner can grow mold, and EPA says touching or breathing mold can cause allergic reactions, including a skin rash, in sensitive people ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home)). Why it happens and how to stop it is in [mold and moss on artificial turf](/guides/does-artificial-turf-get-moldy/).

## Can you be allergic to artificial turf itself?

We couldn't find a published case. A [PubMed search](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=artificial+turf+dermatitis) on September 25, 2026 for artificial turf dermatitis found no results, and so did searches for artificial or synthetic turf allergy and artificial turf urticaria. That isn't proof it never happens, so here is what's in turf to ask about:

- **The blades:** the polyethylene, polypropylene or nylon named above.
- **The backing coating.** Washington's health department says it can be latex or polyurethane, and that people using turf fields "aren't likely to be exposed to backing material," while the workers who install and remove turf handle it. The Synthetic Turf Council's 2013 buyer's guide lists polyurethane, hot melt polyolefin and latex, and said North American makers were using the first two ([STC](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.syntheticturfcouncil.org/resource/resmgr/guidelines/STC_Considerations_When_Buyi.pdf)). Latex allergy is a reaction to proteins in natural rubber latex, and AAAAI notes that latex paints contain none and synthetic latex gloves contain no natural latex ([AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/latex-allergy)). "Latex" on a spec sheet doesn't settle it; ask which kind.
- **Crumb rubber infill:** shredded recycled tires, per Washington's health department. The American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology lists rubber among common causes of contact dermatitis. In [Firestone](/areas/firestone-co/), the town's turf standard prohibits crumb rubber infill ([Firestone Development Code 16.6.4](https://library.municode.com/co/firestone/codes/municipal_code?nodeId=TIT16FIDECO_CH6DEDEST_16.6.4LASCFE)); not legal advice. The rest of the crumb rubber question is in [is crumb rubber safe](/guides/is-crumb-rubber-safe/).
- **Plant infills:** Washington's health department says some studies found them lowest in chemicals of concern but a possible source of "mold, allergens, and inhalation of fibers." Motz, which sells a walnut-shell infill, says a heating process removes residual walnut allergens from the shells ([Motz](https://themotzgroup.com/infill_blog/organic-infill-can-make-your-artificial-turf-more-sustainable-7-things-to-know/)). That is the maker's claim, not an independent test. With a tree-nut allergy in the house, ask the allergist before choosing it.

Chemical questions, PFAS included, are in [is artificial turf toxic](/guides/is-artificial-turf-toxic/).

## What else causes a rash after time on turf?

Usually something other than an allergy to the turf:

- **Turf burn.** Washington's health department calls it a skin abrasion from friction against the fibers, and recommends long sleeves, long pants and long socks for sliding games.
- **Heat.** The same agency says hot turf raises concerns of burns and blisters. See [how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/).
- **What else touched the skin.** ACAAI lists sunscreen, fragrant soaps and bleach among common causes of contact dermatitis, and says a rash can appear from a few hours to 10 days after contact ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/skin-allergy/contact-dermatitis/)). A cleaner or disinfectant used on the turf belongs on that list.
- **Mold or bites.** Mold on damp debris is covered above; ants, fleas and other bugs are in [does artificial turf attract bugs](/guides/does-artificial-turf-attract-bugs/).

To find the cause, ACAAI suggests listing anything that touched your skin in the two days before the rash, and says an allergist may patch test for causes such as rubber. Bring the turf's spec sheet to that appointment.

## What should I ask before choosing turf for an allergic household?

| Ask | A good answer |
|---|---|
| What are the blades, the backing coating and the infill made of? | Named materials from the product's spec sheet, in writing |
| Is there natural rubber latex anywhere in it? | The coating named, with the maker's statement to back it |
| Which infill would you use, and what else could go in? | A named infill and a named alternative, with the reason for each |
| Can I have the spec sheet and a sample before I sign? | Yes, so an allergist can see what it is made of |
| How should we rinse it, and how often? | A routine that fits your town's watering rules |

More on reading a spec sheet is in [what a turf spec sheet should say](/guides/turf-spec-sheet-for-colorado/), and the wider list is in [questions to ask a turf installer](/guides/questions-to-ask-turf-installer/).
