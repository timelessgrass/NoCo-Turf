faq:
  - q: "Can road salt or magnesium chloride kill artificial turf?"
    a: "None of the turf makers we read say it does. FieldTurf says magnesium chloride won't harm turf beyond corroding any exposed metal, and that regular salt dries into crystals rough enough to scuff fibers. SYNLawn says rock salt and chemical de-icers can degrade fibers and backing, and Synthetic Grass Warehouse says salt can plug the drainage holes in the backing. The beds, trees and concrete around the turf are the likelier casualties."
  - q: "Is a pet-safe ice melt also safe for my trees and concrete?"
    a: "Not necessarily. The ASPCA says pet-friendly melts typically contain urea or magnesium chloride. CSU Extension ties magnesium chloride to tree damage, the National Ready Mixed Concrete Association says never to put magnesium-based salts on concrete, and WJE lists urea as possibly damaging to plants. The label speaks to one risk; the ingredient list tells you about the rest."
  - q: "Is a salt-free ice melt really free of salt?"
    a: "Read the bag. Nebraska Extension calls calcium magnesium acetate a de-icer that contains no salt, but WJE says many CMA products are sold blended with chloride salts. Pet Poison Helpline also files CMA with the calcium salts it rates the harshest on a dog's skin and paws."
  - q: "My tree's leaves browned at the edges in July. Could last winter's ice melt do that?"
    a: "It could. CSU Extension says chloride damage starts at leaf edges and needle tips, can take months after leaf-out to appear, and looks a lot like drought, winter burn or some herbicides. CSU's way to tell them apart is a lab test of the leaves for chloride. Keeping a tree watered under turf is covered in our guide to watering trees after turf goes in."
  - q: "Who decides what goes on my street, the city or CDOT?"
    a: "It depends on the road. CDOT plows state highways and interstates, and each city or town plows its own streets. If your yard fronts a state highway, CDOT's salt and magnesium chloride products are in the slush the plows throw. On a city street, ask the city's streets department what it spreads."

Whether you can spread ice melt on turf, and how to clear snow without hurting it, is in our [guide to snow removal on turf](/guides/artificial-turf-snow-removal/). This page is about the salt that gets into a turf yard anyway: off your walk, off the driveway, and in the slush a plow throws from the street. The turf makers we read warn about salt on the fibers and backing; the beds, trees and concrete beside the turf can come off worse.

## Where does the salt in a turf yard come from?

Three places, and you control only one of them.

- **Your own walk and driveway.** CSU Extension says granulated magnesium chloride is commonly spread in small amounts on sidewalks, driveways and walkways ([CSU Fact Sheet 7.425](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/magnesium-chloride-toxicity-in-trees/)). Whatever you spread leaves with the snow you shovel, and cities want that snow in the yard: Fort Collins says to shovel it into your yard, not the street ([City of Fort Collins](https://www.fortcollins.gov/Services/Transportation-and-Roads/Snow-Removal)), and Longmont asks people on plowed streets to put sidewalk snow on their own property ([City of Longmont](https://longmontcolorado.gov/transportation/snow-ice-control/)).
- **The city street.** Fort Collins applies salt brine to roads before a storm so snow can't bond to the pavement. Longmont de-ices with Meltdown Apex and Ice Slicer and anti-ices with a sodium chloride brine. What Loveland and Windsor use is in the snow removal guide.
- **A state highway.** CDOT plows state highways and interstates; cities and towns plow their own roads. Every CDOT snow product contains salt with corrosion inhibitors. Its solid de-icer, Ice Slicer, is granular salt and magnesium chloride, and on pavement below 16°F it uses a cold-temperature magnesium chloride liquid. During a storm it de-ices only highways carrying more than 15,000 vehicles a day; afterward, any highway may get it ([CDOT](https://www.codot.gov/travel/winter-driving/snow-removal-and-safety)).

Then the salt travels. CSU's GardenNotes on saline soils says road salt reaches plants by two routes: down into the soil to the roots, and through the air as splash-back from passing traffic onto stems and leaves ([CSU GardenNotes #224](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/saline-soils/)). CSU's magnesium chloride fact sheet adds that along treated highways the splash zone may stretch 300 feet, and that most of the damage turns up on trees downhill of the road edge. Driveways collect it too: the National Ready Mixed Concrete Association says the magnesium salts used to pretreat roads get tracked onto concrete by cars ([NRMCA](https://www.nrmca.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/02pr.pdf)).

## Which ice melt is easiest on turf, plants, concrete and paws?

None is easy on all four. Here is what each source we read says, ingredient by ingredient:

| Ice melt | The turf | Plants beside it | Concrete | Dogs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rock salt (sodium chloride) | Crystals scuff fibers (FieldTurf); can plug the backing's holes (Synthetic Grass Warehouse) | Damages many kinds (WJE); hurts soil structure at high levels (Chicago Botanic Garden) | Acceptable to NRMCA, though it can still scale (WJE) | A common ice melt salt; dogs mostly take it in by licking paws or eating treated snow (ASPCA) |
| Calcium chloride | Preferred over rock salt on fields, but leaves a residue (FieldTurf) | Somewhat less harmful than rock salt (WJE) | Acceptable to NRMCA; Chicago Botanic Garden calls it highly corrosive to concrete | Calcium salts are the harshest irritants, paws included (Pet Poison Helpline) |
| Magnesium chloride | Won't harm the turf, apart from corroding exposed metal (FieldTurf) | Its chloride burns leaf edges and needle tips (CSU) | Never use it, NRMCA says; scales more than sodium or calcium chloride (WJE) | A usual "pet-friendly" ingredient that can still upset a stomach (ASPCA) |
| Potassium acetate | Not addressed by the turf makers we read | Acetates are the safer pick near salt-sensitive plants (NASA Langley) | May cause alkali-silica reaction (WJE) | Not named by the two pet sources we read |
| CMA (calcium magnesium acetate) | Not addressed by the turf makers we read | Little or no damage (Chicago Botanic Garden); not expected to harm plants (WJE) | Sources split: no damage (Nebraska Extension) or more scaling than rock salt (WJE) | Filed with the calcium salts (Pet Poison Helpline) |

FieldTurf writes about sports fields; we apply it to a yard.*

Two things stand out. The de-icer FieldTurf says won't harm turf, magnesium chloride, is the one CSU links to tree damage and NRMCA says never to put on concrete. And the gentlest on plants, CMA, is grouped with the harshest ingredients for paws, and WJE says many CMA products are blended with chloride salts, so read the ingredients. WJE's own summary is that no de-icer is harmless to every material, so the choice depends on what sits beside the pavement.

In practice, use less of whatever you buy. Nebraska Extension says de-icers are there to make shoveling easier, not to melt everything, and to get the salt off the concrete as soon as you can. Where grip is all you need, CSU suggests sand or another abrasive on slick walks; Nebraska adds that sand won't melt ice.

## What does the runoff do to the trees and beds next to the turf?

It soaks into the soil, and the roots drink it. CSU says magnesium chloride moves from treated roads into the soil beside them with precipitation, and trees take the magnesium and chloride up through their roots into their leaves. The chloride collects at the edges of leaves and needles, so that is where the damage begins: browned margins on deciduous leaves, burned tips on conifer needles, spreading as more chloride builds up.

Turf doesn't stop it. Turf is built to let water through: Synthetic Grass Warehouse describes snowmelt draining through the perforated backing. So salty meltwater on the turf keeps going, down through the base into the soil, which is where a yard tree's roots are (our reading of how turf drains, not a test of it). How far those roots spread under a turf yard is in our guide to [artificial turf around trees](/guides/artificial-turf-around-trees/).

Expect a delay. CSU says a deciduous tree may not show symptoms for several months after it leafs out, and that salt injury is generally worse in hot, dry weather, when plants use the most water. January's salt can show up as brown leaf edges in July. Drought, winter burn and some herbicides look much the same, CSU adds, and water stress makes chloride damage worse; a lab test of the leaves for chloride is how to tell. Watering a tree once turf covers its roots is covered in [watering trees after turf goes in](/guides/watering-trees-with-artificial-turf/).

Where to look first: CSU says to expect salt problems in the soil beside salted walks and in the swales where snowmelt runs. In a turf yard that usually means the bed along the driveway, the strip between the sidewalk and the street, and any low corner that collects meltwater.

## Will ice melt damage the concrete border around my turf?

It can, and a new border is the most exposed. NRMCA defines scaling as the flaking or peeling of a concrete surface from freeze-thaw cycles, and says de-icers make it worse by saturating the surface and adding cycles. Salt solution that sits on concrete because it can't drain makes the exposure more severe.

- **A curb, walk or patio poured this year.** NRMCA says to avoid de-icers on newly placed concrete where possible, and through its first winter, and to use clean sand for traction. If your turf went in this year with a new concrete edge, that edge is in its first winter now.
- **Magnesium-based products.** Never on concrete, says NRMCA. WJE says magnesium chloride and CMA can both scale concrete more than sodium or calcium chloride, and that potassium acetate may cause alkali-silica reaction.
- **Road salt on the driveway.** NRMCA says to hose off the salt cars leave on driveways and garage slabs when conditions permit. Our guide to [cleaning turf](/guides/how-to-clean-artificial-turf/) covers what may run off a yard into the gutter.

Bender board, metal, concrete curb and pavers, and how each edge holds through freeze-thaw, are compared in [artificial turf edging options](/guides/artificial-turf-edging-options/).

## Is salty snow on the turf a problem for my dog?

The pile is. The ASPCA says pets usually take in ice melt by licking their feet after walking through it or by eating snow that holds it, and says not to let dogs eat treated snow, especially slush. The heap of shoveled snow on the turf beside the walk is exactly that snow.

- **Pile it away from the dog's spot.** Put the walk's snow on rock or in a corner the dog doesn't use.
- **Clean paws after the walk.** The ASPCA's advice is a damp cloth or baby wipes as soon as the dog comes in, and paw wax or booties for pads that crack or bleed.
- **Don't trust a label alone.** Pet Poison Helpline counts CMA among the calcium salts, the most severe irritants of any ice melt ingredient, on skin and paws as well as swallowed, and says every type of ice melt can be hazardous.

More on turf and pets is in [is artificial turf safe for pets](/guides/is-artificial-turf-safe-for-pets/), and how a dog area is built is on our [pet turf](/services/pet-turf/) page.

## How do I rinse the salt out in spring?

Wait for a real thaw, then do the turf and the beds separately.

- **The turf.** FieldTurf's rule for fields is to flush with water once it has been above freezing for five days in a row and the forecast looks good; it also warns that some de-icers leave a sticky residue. SYNLawn's spring routine starts with a garden-hose rinse of what built up over the winter ([SYNLawn](https://www.synlawn.com/articles/how-to-maintain-artificial-grass/)). Spend the water on the strip along the walk and driveway and wherever a snow pile sat.
- **The beds.** CSU says leaching with clean water is the only practical way to get excess salt out of soil. One slow, continuous watering of 6 inches leaches about half the salt, 12 inches about four-fifths and 24 inches about nine-tenths, and it works only if the water soaks in and drains below the roots instead of running off. Clay, compaction or hardpan can keep salty soil from being reclaimed at all, which matters in a clay yard.
- **The trees.** CSU says flushing can clear magnesium chloride from the upper soil, though a tree's wide-spreading roots may benefit less than a crop's. University of Arizona Extension says to irrigate, in spring, the areas where de-icers were used heavily ([University of Arizona Extension](https://www.extension.arizona.edu/sites/extension.arizona.edu/files/attachment/Deicers.pdf)).

Before a long soak, check what your water provider allows; [do you have to water artificial turf](/guides/do-you-have-to-water-artificial-turf/) goes through the hose rules.

## How should a new turf yard be laid out for salt?

Give the salt somewhere to land that isn't a root zone.

- **Rock between the pavement and the plants.** CSU says salt from de-icing can climb past what even salt-tolerant plants handle, so a rock mulch area without plants may be the better design where salt gets used. Along a walk or driveway that suggests a band of rock first, then the turf, then the beds and trees.
- **Snow piles on rock, never on beds.** Nebraska Extension says not to pile snow onto trees, shrubs or flower gardens, and University of Arizona Extension says not to pile snow carrying de-icer on or near salt-sensitive plants. Chicago Botanic Garden suggests spreading out snow from treated surfaces so what's left in it is diluted.
- **Water off the pavement.** Chicago Botanic Garden says to set downspouts and grades so they don't spill onto walks or drives, and to pitch pavement so meltwater drains instead of pooling. Arizona suggests gutters or barriers that steer de-icing runoff away from planting areas.
- **Sensitive plants away from the road.** Arizona says to keep salt-sensitive plants away from where de-icer is spread and splashed. On a lot beside a state highway, remember the splash zone CSU describes, and that the side downhill of the road takes most of it.

How to split a yard among turf, rock and beds is in [turf, rock and plant beds](/guides/mixing-turf-rock-and-plant-beds/), and front yards that meet the street are in [front yard artificial turf ideas](/guides/front-yard-artificial-turf-ideas/). When you plan [artificial turf installation](/services/artificial-turf-installation/), ask the installer to mark on the drawing where the walk and driveway snow will go, which edge meets a plowed street, and what sits downhill of it.
