  question: "Can backyard chickens live on artificial turf?"
  answer: "Not in their run. Chickens spend much of the day scratching and pecking at the ground and need loose dirt to dust-bathe, and CSU Extension says to give them soil or sand. Turf offers neither, and droppings stay on it, because turf doesn't break waste down the way a living lawn does. Keep the run on soil or sand, and use turf for the lawn beyond it."
faq:
  - q: "How close can turf come to the coop and run?"
    a: "Turf can meet the run's edge; what the codes control is where the coop and run sit. Fort Collins keeps the poultry house and its enclosure at least 15 feet from abutting property lines unless those neighbors consent in writing. Longmont keeps a coop behind the house and at least 6 feet from other structures and from side and rear lot lines. Not legal advice."
  - q: "Do chicken droppings make artificial turf smell?"
    a: "Left in place, they can. CSU Extension says poultry manure is particularly high in ammonia, and its poultry brochure calls for coop ventilation to keep ammonia from building up. Lift droppings when you see them and rinse the spot. A smell can also become a neighbor's complaint: Loveland counts an animal that creates offensive odors as a public nuisance, and Longmont requires coops to be cleaned regularly to control dust, odor and waste."
  - q: "Can kids play on turf where the hens roam?"
    a: "Give them turf the hens don't use. The CDC says children younger than 5 shouldn't handle or touch poultry, advises washing hands after touching anything in the area where poultry live and roam, and says not to eat or drink there. Fence the play turf and the patio off from the hens' area, and have everyone wash with soap and water after time in the yard."
  - q: "Can coop litter and droppings go in the compost?"
    a: "Yes, says CSU Extension, if you have an active compost pile. Otherwise use the material in non-food landscaped areas or take it to a commercial compost facility. Keep fresh manure out of a food garden, since it may carry salmonella, and keep it off the turf and out of the gutter."
  - q: "Why do so many towns ban roosters?"
    a: "Noise, mostly. The CDC says many cities have rules against owning roosters because their crowing violates noise ordinances. Fort Collins allows only hens under its backyard poultry rules, and Longmont bans roosters outside agricultural zoning. Loveland's animal code doesn't name roosters, but its noise rule covers any animal noise that is excessive, continuous or untimely."
  - q: "What should I ask an installer about turf beside a chicken run?"
    a: "Ask where the turf's rinse water drains and where the run's runoff goes in a storm, so neither crosses the other. Ask what edge will keep sand from the run out of the turf, and how hot the turf next to the run gets in afternoon sun. A good answer marks the run on the plan before the base goes in."

Hens and artificial turf can share a backyard, but not the same ground. A chicken's day is spent working the soil, and turf is built so there's no soil to work. This page covers where turf fits around a flock, how to clean droppings off it, and the hen rules in Fort Collins, Loveland, Greeley and Longmont. Dog waste has [its own guide](/guides/dog-poop-on-artificial-turf/).

## Should a chicken run be artificial turf?

No. A run's floor is where hens do most of what hens do, and turf takes it away.

- **They forage by scratching.** Alabama Cooperative Extension (Auburn University) says chickens spend around 61 percent of their active time foraging and feeding, "pecking and scratching at the ground to find food sources." A chicken that can't forage can become frustrated and turn to feather pecking, egg eating and cannibalism ([Alabama Extension](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/poultry/common-backyard-chicken-behaviors/)).
- **They bathe in dirt.** A dust-bathing hen digs a small hollow, rolls in the dirt and shakes it back out of her feathers. Kept from it, chickens pace, peck at each other and go through "sham dust bathing (imitation dust bathing when no substrate is present)." A CSU Extension brochure lists dust baths, with a clean coop, among what limits lice and mites ([CSU Extension brochure](https://boulder.extension.colostate.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2020/04/Poultry-brochure-2020.pdf)).
- **CSU Extension's advice is soil or sand.** It suggests about 10 square feet per chicken in a coop or run, and says to "make sure they have access to soil or sand" ([CSU Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/poultry/)).

Turf gives a scratching hen plastic blades with infill, the sand or granules between them, on a backing fastened down over a base. There's nothing to find and nothing to bathe in. And what lands on it stays: CSU's PlantTalk Colorado, writing about pet waste, says artificial surfaces "do not naturally disinfect themselves by biological means like a living lawn does" ([PlantTalk 1537](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/lawns/1537-artificial-turf-lawns/)).

A run floor also has to come out now and then. The CSU brochure says run material "will need cleaned out to help avoid smell, contamination." Sand or soil can be raked out and replaced. Turf is fastened to its base and stays.

## Can hens free-range on a turf lawn?

They can visit it. They will still scratch and peck, because exploring, for a chicken, means "pecking at objects and the environment with the beak and scratching the floor," as the Alabama Extension page puts it. On turf, that's the blades and the infill. The bigger question is who else uses that lawn.

The CDC's backyard poultry guidance ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/about/backyard-poultry.html)) says to:

- wash your hands after touching poultry "or anything in the area where they live and roam";
- "Don't eat or drink in areas where poultry live or roam";
- keep poultry out of the house, including "outdoor patios" where food or drink is served;
- set aside a pair of shoes for poultry chores and keep them outside the house;
- keep children younger than 5 from handling or touching poultry.

So a turf lawn the hens range on is a hen area, not the picnic lawn or the play turf. If the yard has one lawn, decide which job it does. If the hens share it, pick up after every visit, before people use it again.

Longmont settles the question for its residents: backyard hens "may not free range and are restricted to the coop and run" unless every abutting property owner gives written permission. The other three cities' rules are below.

## How do you clean chicken droppings off artificial turf?

Lift them; don't rinse them in. The CDC says outbreaks of Salmonella linked to backyard poultry are common, that poultry shed germs in their droppings, and that poultry "can appear healthy and clean, but still spread harmful germs that make people sick."

1. **Gloves on.** The CDC says to wear work or utility gloves to clean droppings, never to pick them up bare-handed, and to wash your hands afterwards.
2. **Lift the solids first,** with a scoop or a bag. The pickup routine is the same one [dog owners use on turf](/guides/dog-poop-on-artificial-turf/).
3. **Rinse, then disinfect if you need to.** The CDC's steps for poultry equipment carry over: get droppings and dirt off with soap and warm water first, because "most disinfectants only work on clean surfaces," then leave the disinfectant on for the label's time, usually 30 seconds to 10 minutes, rinse and let it dry. Which disinfectants a turf label allows, and why bleach is out, are in [how to clean artificial turf](/guides/how-to-clean-artificial-turf/).
4. **Keep the rinse water in the yard,** soaking down through the turf rather than running to the street. The same cleaning guide quotes the city stormwater rules.
5. **Clean the tools outdoors.** The CDC says to clean anything used to care for poultry outside, never in the house.

Two more things about chicken droppings:

- **Nitrogen.** CSU Extension says poultry manure "is particularly high in ammonia and readily burns if over-applied," more so fresh than aged. Its manure table puts poultry manure at 1.7 to 2.8 percent nitrogen, against 1.1 percent for beef and 0.7 percent for horse manure ([CSU GardenNotes #242](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/using-manure-in-colorado-gardens/)). Don't hose a pile off the turf into a planting bed.
- **Where it goes.** The CSU brochure says coop and run waste can go on an active compost pile, in non-food landscaped areas or to a commercial compost facility, but fresh manure doesn't belong in a food garden because it "may carry diseases such as salmonella."

## What should go in the coop and run instead?

Litter in the coop, soil or sand in the run. From the CSU Extension sources above:

**In the coop**

- A deep litter of pine (not cedar) shavings on the floor. The brochure notes a concrete floor is easier to disinfect than wood or dirt.
- Roosts with 6 to 12 inches per bird, over a screen with a removable area below, which keeps the coop cleaner. Chickens "poop a lot at night," CSU says, so nest boxes don't go under the roosts.
- Ventilation, so ammonia doesn't build up, especially in hot months.

**In the run**

- Soil or sand underfoot, with 6 to 10 square feet per bird depending on breed size.
- A floor you can clean out, so it doesn't smell or spread contamination.
- A well-drained spot, knowing where runoff goes in a big storm. Pick one whose runoff won't cross the turf or the patio.
- Shade. The brochure suggests shade cloth over the run in the hot months. The nearest NOAA station to Greeley averages 58.3 days a year at or above 90°F, and the Fort Collins station 29.5 (1991 to 2020 normals). Turf beside the run in full sun gets hot too; see [how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/).
- A cover against hawks and owls, and fencing buried up to 12 inches deep against diggers.

**Where the run meets the turf**

A dust-bathing hen "shakes all the substrate from its feathers," and scratching throws more. Sand that leaves the run ends up in the turf. Give the run a solid edge between the sand and the turf. [Turf edging options](/guides/artificial-turf-edging-options/) compares the edges.

- **Fort Collins:** these hen rules apply in zones where farm animals aren't otherwise allowed.
