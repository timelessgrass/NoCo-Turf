  answer: "Yes, if the ground around each cup ends up nearly flat. faq:
  - q: "Does Firestone's 5:1 turf slope limit apply to a putting green?"
    a: "Firestone's code bars artificial turf on slopes steeper than 5:1, which is a 20 percent slope, and writes no exception for putting greens. A playable green is far flatter than that, so the line matters for the fringe and banks of a green cut into a hillside. It depends how the town reads it for your plan: ask Firestone Planning, and get the town's turf permit before work starts. Not legal advice."
  - q: "Should the cups go at the top or the bottom of the slope?"
    a: "Neither edge, ideally. A cup with a slope running down away from it punishes every putt from above, and USGA guidance wants a golfer above the hole to be able to putt boldly, not just defensively. Put cups on the flattest pockets and let the slope sit between them, where it makes break instead of runaway putts."
  - q: "Can more break be added after the green is built?"
    a: "Only by lifting the turf. Break is shaped in the base underneath, so changing it means pulling the turf back, reshaping and recompacting, then relaying it. Settle the break and the cup spots on the bare base, before any turf goes down."
  - q: "Is a sloped green more work to look after?"
    a: "A little. After a hard rain, sand infill can drift toward the low side, and on a putting green the sand level helps set the speed, so the two ends can roll differently until it is brushed back and topped up."
  - q: "Do I need a retaining wall to put a green on a hillside?"
    a: "Sometimes, but that is a hillside question more than a putting one, about walls, permits for them, town limits on turf slopes and water running down from above."

A sloped backyard can hold a putting green. Whether it plays well comes down to the slope right around each cup, and how much of that a green can take depends on how fast the ball rolls. The numbers below come from the USGA Green Section Record and were written for golf-course greens; a ball obeys the same physics on synthetic turf.

## How much slope can the ground around a cup have?

Less than most people expect. The USGA's Green Section has long suggested keeping most of a putting green at 3 percent slope or less, allowing steeper spots only for hard terrain or a deliberate design effect ([Lemons, USGA Green Section Record, 2008](https://gsrpdf.lib.msu.edu/?file=/2000s/2008/080721.pdf)). Around the cups the same article is stricter:

- On a green that reads 10 feet on the Stimpmeter, any slope of 3 percent or more is too steep for a hole.
- The two to three feet around a hole should be as close to level as possible and of one even grade, though it doesn't have to be dead level.
- At least 3 feet around a cup should hold one consistent slope. Where the slope is steeper, make that 6 feet.
- From anywhere on the green, a golfer should be able to stop the ball within about two feet of the hole.

The faster the green, the flatter those cup areas have to be: the article's chart shifts the safe band toward gentler slopes as speed rises. The rest of the green can carry more tilt. A 1997 Green Section Record analysis found that greens rolling 8 to 12 feet start to rattle golfers where they tilt more than 3 to 4 degrees, about 5 to 7 percent ([Weber, USGA Green Section Record, 1997](https://gsrpdf.lib.msu.edu/?file=/1990s/1997/970312.pdf)).

For scale, 3 percent is about three-eighths of an inch of drop per foot, or a little under 4 inches across a 10-foot run.

## How does slope change the speed of a putt?

Downhill far more than uphill. Weber's model converts a green's speed on the flat into how far the same Stimpmeter roll travels on a slope:

| Speed on the flat (Stimpmeter, feet) | Down a 1° slope (about 1.7%) | Down a 2° slope (about 3.5%) | Up a 2° slope | Slope where a rolling ball never stops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.0 | 6.71 | 7.62 | 4.94 | about 17% |
| 8.5 | 9.98 | 12.1 | 6.51 | about 11% |
| 11.0 | 13.8 | 18.2 | 7.93 | about 9% |

Source: Weber, USGA Green Section Record, March/April 1997. Degrees converted to percent.

Two things follow. A green that rolls 11 on the flat plays like 18.2 down a 3.5 percent slope, which is why a cup sitting below a slope that size gets putts running well past it. And the slope where a ball physically can't stop is several times steeper than the slope where putting stops being fair, so "the ball stays put" is the wrong test for a cup spot.

A 1990 lab test showed the same on synthetic carpet. On a tilted runway covered in patio grass carpet that rolled about 8 or 9 feet, downhill readings took off once the slope passed 1 or 2 percent, while uphill readings dropped only slowly ([Brede, USGA Green Section Record, 1990](https://gsrpdf.lib.msu.edu/?file=/1990s/1990/901110.pdf)). So choose the speed first; our guide to [putting green stimp speed](/guides/putting-green-stimp-speed/) covers what a backyard green should run.

## How do I measure the slope in my yard?

Not by eye. In that same 1990 article, most of the students Brede tested could not tell a 2.2 percent slope from level.

- **A level on a straight board.** Lay a straight 8- or 10-foot board on the ground, set a 4-foot level on it, lift the low end until the bubble centers, and measure the gap under the board. Divide the gap by the board's length in feet for the drop per foot. A quarter inch per foot is about 2 percent, three-eighths about 3 percent, half an inch about 4.
- **A string line** for longer runs: stake a level string across the yard and measure down to the ground every few feet to map the high and low sides.
- **The ball test.** Once the base is shaped, roll a ball from above each planned cup. If it speeds up as it passes the hole, that spot may not work. A Green Section rule of thumb puts each hole in the middle of a 3-foot circle that lies on one plane ([Happ, USGA Green Section Record, 2000](https://gsrpdf.lib.msu.edu/?file=/2000s/2000/000106.pdf)).
- **Up and down rolls.** The USGA's course rating guide, as quoted in the 2008 article, calls a green moderately sloped when a downhill Stimpmeter roll goes twice as far as the uphill one, and steeply sloped at three times.

## What if my yard is steeper than that?

Then the green stops following the yard, and the base under it gets reshaped. Soil comes off the high side and goes onto the low side, compacted, so each cup sits in a pocket close to the slope you chose. The yard's extra fall goes somewhere nobody putts: the fringe, a bank, a low wall, or a step between two tiers of green.

A 10-foot stretch of 5 percent ground falls 6 inches. At 3 percent it would fall a little under 4, so a cup area there needs 2 or more inches of cut or fill end to end before any break is added on purpose.

Golf courses fix greens that are too steep the same way. A 2003 Green Section Record case study describes greens with slopes over 8 percent that, rolling above 10 on the Stimpmeter, had only two or three usable hole positions. The club raised the fronts with soil, in places 9 to 14 inches, lowered the backs up to 4 inches, and called compacting that soil against settling a critically important step ([Graves and Kennelly, USGA Green Section Record, 2003](https://gsrpdf.lib.msu.edu/?file=/2000s/2003/030918.pdf)).

## What changes next to the house and on Northern Colorado clay?

Two things work against a flat green here.

**The ground by the foundation has to fall.** The 2021 International Residential Code requires the grade to drop at least 6 inches within the first 10 feet from the foundation, a 5 percent slope, or drains and swales where the lot can't manage that ([IRC R401.3, UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/colorado/irc-2021/chapter/4/foundations)). That is steeper than a cup should sit, so the cups belong beyond that first 10 feet, with an apron or fringe taking the fall. Towns adopt their own code editions and amendments, so ask your town's building department which applies; this is not legal advice. The Colorado Geological Survey also lists poorly managed surface drainage near foundations among the landscaping problems on expansive soils.

**Clay moves.** The Colorado Geological Survey says expansive clays can swell up to 20 percent by volume when wet ([CGS](https://coloradogeologicalsurvey.org/hazards/expansive-soil-rock/)). Federal soil surveys rate the Nunn and Weld soils common around Loveland and Berthoud as limited by shrink-swell. The soil unit mapped most widely around central Berthoud is Nunn clay loam on 1 to 3 percent slopes: ground that is already near the range a green can use, on a clay that can settle or heave a carefully set cup pocket out of true. How the fill is compacted matters as much as the number on the plan.

## What should I ask an installer about slope?

- **What slope will the cup areas have, and how will you measure it?** A good answer is a number in percent, checked with a level, that fits the speed you want: at 10 on the Stimpmeter, under 3 percent, with a few feet of even slope around each cup.
- **What speed will the green run?** Slope and speed are set together. A speed picked for a flat green, laid over a tilted cup area, plays too fast downhill.
- **Where does the yard's extra fall go?** Look for it on the plan as fringe, bank, wall or tier, not spread under the cups.
- **How is fill placed and compacted on clay?** Listen for compaction in layers and a base written into the quote. Our guide to [checking the base was installed](/guides/check-turf-base-was-installed/) shows how to confirm it afterward.
- **Can I roll balls on the shaped base before the turf goes down?** A good answer is yes. That is the easy moment to move a cup.
