Artificial turf is plastic, and on nearly every spec sheet the plastic is one of three: polyethylene, polypropylene or nylon. It's easy to treat that as one choice for the whole yard. It is really several. The blades of a lawn, the curly thatch at their base and a putting surface do different jobs, and makers often use a different yarn for each.

## Which yarn goes where in a turf yard?

Polyethylene on top in most lawns, polyethylene or polypropylene in the thatch, and all three on putting greens.

| Part of the yard | Yarns on published spec sheets | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Lawn blades | Polyethylene in most products; nylon in a few | [SYNLawn SYNAugustine 847](https://www.synlawn.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SYNLawn-SYNAugustine-847-S847B-USA.pdf): polyethylene. [Synthetic Grass Warehouse Nylon Premier Lawn](https://sgw-media.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/assets/spec-sheets/nylon-premier-lawn-ss.pdf): 100% nylon |
| Thatch (the short, curled layer under the blades) | Polyethylene or polypropylene | SYNAugustine 847: polyethylene thatch. [SYNLawn SYNCanine](https://www.synlawn.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/SYNLawn-SYNCanine-SWN60-USA.pdf): polypropylene thatch under polyethylene blades |
| Putting surface | Nylon, polyethylene or polypropylene | One supplier sells a 0.50-inch green in each (see the table further down) |

AstroTurf's glossary says polyethylene is "generally more commonly used in synthetic turf," and that polypropylene "is typically employed in specific applications where cost-effectiveness and lighter weight are priorities" ([AstroTurf](https://astroturf.com/synthetic-turf-glossary-of-terms/)). Polypropylene is also the yarn of indoor-outdoor carpet: the University of Southern Mississippi's Polymer Science Learning Center points to the carpet "around swimming pools and miniature golf courses," and notes that polypropylene doesn't absorb water the way nylon does ([PSLC](https://pslc.ws/macrog/pp.htm)).

Putting greens and dog runs sit outside the trade group's lawn advice. The Synthetic Turf Council's landscape guidelines say they "may not apply" to putting greens or pet areas ([Synthetic Turf Council](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.syntheticturfcouncil.org/resource/resmgr/guidelines/STC_Considerations_When_Buyi.pdf)), so judge those products on their own sheets.

## Is nylon tougher than polyethylene?

For wear, generally yes. AstroTurf calls nylon "the dominant fiber choice for commercial carpet use due to its wear characteristics," and says nylon turf fibers are "resistant to abrasion." It also says nylon is "typically used in specialized applications." A patent application from a turf company developing an all-nylon grass describes the weakness nylon is meant to fix: polyethylene and polypropylene blades that stay pressed down "for a significant period of time" may "tend to retain their deformed shape" ([patent application US20210040698A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20210040698A1/en)). That is the flattening owners call matting; fixing it is in our [guide to matted turf](/guides/matted-artificial-turf/).

Polyethylene's case is feel. AstroTurf says polyethylene fibers "can be engineered to have a softer texture." A Lankhorst yarn patent notes that blade-to-skin friction has to stay low "to avoid skin-burns," and that linear low-density polyethylene "has a lower coefficient of friction than polypropylene" ([U.S. Patent US7611763B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7611763B2/en)). Dow's own turf-yarn patent names the catch: conventional polyethylene turf yarns "are soft, yet unfortunately lack heat resistance" ([U.S. Patent US9040627B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9040627B2/en)).

Before paying for a polymer, weigh the trade group's caution. The Synthetic Turf Council tells buyers that polyethylene, nylon and polypropylene "can provide" strength and durability, elastic recovery and softness, among other features. The name of the plastic doesn't settle how a product wears; its test data does.

Toughness earns its cost where the wear concentrates, such as a dog's run along the fence or the area around a green's cups. An open backyard lawn that gets bare feet and a lawn chair asks for less.

## Which yarn handles heat and window glare?

Nylon, by a wide margin. Polypropylene has some room. Polyethylene has the least.

| Heat source or limit | Temperature | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Hottest synthetic turf surface in published research, in plain sun on a 98°F day in Provo, Utah | 200°F | [Penn State](https://plantscience.psu.edu/research/centers/ssrc/documents/temperature.pdf/@@download/file/temperature.pdf) |
| Sunlight focused by a double-pane Low-E window | Over 200°F at the focus; readings under 250°F | [NAHB](https://www.nahb.org/-/media/NAHB/advocacy/docs/legal-issues/construction-liability/builder-resources/sunlight-double-paned-low-e-windows-2014.pdf) |
| Polyethylene softens enough to warp | Around 212°F (100°C) | [PSLC](https://pslc.ws/macrog/pp.htm) |
| Polyethylene melts | About 239–275°F | Patent application US20210040698A1 |
| Polypropylene melts | Not below 320°F (160°C) | [PSLC](https://pslc.ws/macrog/pp.htm) |
| Nylon melts | 400°F on one supplier's sheet; 428–509°F by type in the patent application | [Synthetic Grass Warehouse](https://sgw-media.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/assets/spec-sheets/nylon-premier-lawn-ss.pdf); patent application |

Focused glare lands in the range where polyethylene warps and melts, and the Synthetic Turf Council's note on Low-E glass records "random instances of fiber shrinkage and melting of polyethylene fibers." Nylon's figures sit well clear of that range, which is why it is sold for sunny glass: Synthetic Grass Warehouse says its nylon lawn is "built to withstand burns caused by reflective UV light from low E-Windows." The nylon figures come from companies that sell nylon, so ask for the melting point and the test behind it.

Northern Colorado summers add to the load. NOAA's Greeley UNC station averages 58.3 days a year at or above 90°F ([NOAA](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-annualseasonal-1991-2020&stations=USC00053553&format=json)). What plain sun does to turf temperature is in [how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/).

A higher melting point buys margin, not a cure. The window still throws the same beam onto the yard, and screening or shading the glass, or putting a bed where the beam lands, deals with the cause. That work is in our [guide to turf melting near windows](/guides/artificial-turf-melting-from-windows/).

## Does the yarn change how turf holds up in Colorado sun?

Not much by itself. The EPA says UV intensity "increases about 6% per kilometer elevation above sea level" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/learn-about-uv-index)), which works out to roughly 9% more UV along the Northern Colorado corridor than at sea level. The Synthetic Turf Council lists UV resistance among the features all three polymers can provide, and asks for yarn results "validated in report by an Independent Test Lab" after 3,000 hours of UVA testing. What those numbers mean, and how fast turf fades here, is in our [guide to turf fading](/guides/does-artificial-turf-fade/).

## Can I use nylon turf in Firestone or anywhere else in Northern Colorado?

Not in [Firestone](/areas/firestone-co/): its turf standard allows blades only of lead-free polyethylene, polypropylene, a vegetable-based product or a blend, says "Nylon or other plastic shall be prohibited," and requires a tan thatch layer ([Firestone Development Code 16.6.4](https://library.municode.com/co/firestone/codes/municipal_code?nodeId=TIT16FIDECO_CH6DEDEST_16.6.4LASCFE)). Whether that reaches a backyard putting green is taken up in [sand-filled vs non-infilled putting greens](/guides/sand-filled-vs-non-infilled-putting-green/). Other towns' turf rules are in our [guide to artificial turf rules in Northern Colorado](/guides/turf-rules-northern-colorado/), and an HOA's architectural committee can ask for the product sheet; our [HOA approval guide](/guides/hoa-turf-approval/) covers the packet.

Statewide, Colorado has barred installing artificial turf that contains intentionally added PFAS since January 1, 2026 ([SB24-081](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-081)). That applies whichever yarn you pick, so ask for PFAS documentation for the blades, the thatch and the backing. This is general information, not legal advice.

## What should a spec sheet say about the yarn?

Enough to tell you which plastic is in each part, and how the product was tested:

- **The blade yarn and the thatch yarn, listed separately.** SYNLawn's sheets list a "Grass Zone" yarn and a "Thatch Zone" yarn. A sheet that says only "poly" leaves the question open.
- **For nylon sold for heat: the melting point and the test it came from.** A number with no test behind it is a claim.
- **The UV report.** The independent lab results the Synthetic Turf Council asks for.
- **PFAS documentation**, for Colorado's law.
- **In Firestone:** blades and backing from the town's list of materials, no nylon blades, and a tan thatch.
- **For a putting green: the yarn and the sand it expects.** The polymer doesn't settle the sand question on its own, as one supplier's three greens show:

| Synthetic Grass Warehouse green, all about 0.50 inches tall | Yarn | Face weight | Infill the sheet calls for |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Nylon Putt 2-Tone](https://sgw-media.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/assets/spec-sheets/nylon-putt-2tone-ss.pdf) | Texturized nylon | About 36 oz | 2.0 to 3.0 lb per sq ft |
| [Poly Putt 2-Tone Dark](https://sgw-media.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/assets/spec-sheets/poly-putt-2tone-dark-ss.pdf) | Texturized polyethylene | About 50 oz | 1.5 to 2.5 lb per sq ft |
| [Putting Green PP 40](https://sgw-media.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/assets/spec-sheets/putting-green-pp-40-ss.pdf) | Polypropylene monofilament | About 40 oz | 2 to 3 lb per sq ft |

SYNLawn Golf, which sells nylon greens, says nylon greens are "mostly fiber with very little use of sand," while polypropylene greens need their sand "replenished to maintain proper infill levels" ([SYNLawn Golf](https://www.synlawngolf.com/the-nylon-advantage/)). The sheets above show that isn't a rule of the polymer. Which build suits a backyard green is in [sand-filled vs non-infilled putting greens](/guides/sand-filled-vs-non-infilled-putting-green/); what a putting green project involves is on our [putting greens page](/services/putting-greens/). How heavy a lawn should be is a separate question, in [artificial turf face weight](/guides/artificial-turf-face-weight/), and the whole sheet, line by line, is in [a turf spec sheet for Colorado](/guides/turf-spec-sheet-for-colorado/).

When you talk to installers, ask which yarn is in the blades, the thatch and any green, and why. A good answer reads the polymer off the product's sheet and ties it to what that part of the yard has to handle, such as a dog's path or the glass on the south wall.
