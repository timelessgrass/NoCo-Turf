Agility rules are written for trial rings, not backyards, but they are the clearest published numbers for spacing and setting up obstacles. Here is what the AKC, USDAA and NADAC publish, what changes on artificial turf, and what veterinary researchers have found about surfaces and injuries. Whether turf suits your dogs at all is in [artificial turf vs grass for dogs](/guides/artificial-turf-vs-grass-for-dogs/).

## How much room does a backyard agility course need?

Less than a trial ring, if you train pieces of a course rather than whole ones.

| Course | Smallest area the rules allow | Width |
|---|---|---|
| AKC Novice trial | 5,000 sq ft | At least 60 ft |
| AKC Open trial | 6,500 sq ft | At least 60 ft |
| AKC Excellent, Master and Premier | 8,000 sq ft; a 100 by 120 ft ring is "ideal" | At least 60 ft |
| AKC Agility Course Test, the entry-level event | 3,500 sq ft (3,200 for its jumpers tests) | At least 40 ft |
| USDAA at-home full course | Generally 60 by 80 ft or larger | Not set |
| USDAA at-home small and skills courses | Smaller; a skills course can be any size | Not set |

The AKC rows are from its [agility regulations](https://images.akc.org/pdf/rulebooks/REAGIL.pdf). USDAA's at-home program is built for individual and small-group runs, and its skills courses have no minimum size ([USDAA@Home rules](https://www.usdaa.com/athome/publisheddocuments/USDAA@Home!_DogAgilityRegulations.pdf)).

Spacing decides whether a sequence fits. The AKC recommends 15 to 18 feet between obstacles, at least 18 feet before a jump and 21 before a spread jump, and no more than 30 feet between two obstacles in a row, measured along the path of a dog jumping 20 inches. So measure your longest straight run before you buy anything: a line of three jumps at that spacing needs two 18-foot gaps, plus room to take off before the first and land after the last.

## What size are the jumps, weave poles and tunnel?

| Obstacle | AKC trial spec | What other bodies allow | What it means on turf |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bar jump | Bars 4 to 5 ft long that sit loose, on uprights that must be able to fall over | NADAC: plastic or PVC, so a jump a dog hits falls easily | Nothing about a jump is fixed to the ground |
| Weave poles | 40 in tall, 24 in apart center to center, on a non-slip base no higher than 3/4 in, secured so the poles don't move | NADAC: base no more than 1/2 in high, weighted, no stakes at its events. USDAA at home: poles up to 1 in thick and 36 in tall, stuck in the ground 24 in apart | A weighted base sits on the turf; stick-in poles go through it |
| Open tunnel | Openings 25 in across, 10 to 20 ft long, secured so the dog can't move it | NADAC: saddle bags of at least 20 lb of sand or 3 gal of water each | The weight goes on top of the turf, not through it |
| Table | Not needed for home practice | USDAA at home: a pause box on the ground, no more than 4 ft square | A nonslip mat, or a square with marked corners |

Sources: [NADAC's equipment specifications](https://nadac.com/WPsite/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/NADACEquipmentSpecs-11525.pdf) and [USDAA's home substitutions](https://www.usdaa.com/athome/publisheddocuments/USDAA@Home!_DogAgilityObstacleSubstitutions.pdf). Jump heights by the size of the dog are in the questions below.

## Does artificial turf give a dog enough grip for turns and jumps?

It can. The AKC requires a non-slip surface for every trial course, names packed dirt, grass, carpeting and padded matting as acceptable, and requires concrete to be fully matted or padded. It also limits how sharply a course turns a dog into an obstacle, with the running surface in mind.

What researchers have found:

- **Turf is the common training surface after grass.** In a 2022 survey of the handlers of 308 agility dogs by veterinary sports-medicine researchers, 50.8% of the dogs trained on artificial turf and 85.3% on grass. Handlers saw the fewest signs of poor performance on grass and dirt ([Frontiers in Veterinary Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9624126/)).
- **Handlers blame turf more often than grass.** About a quarter of competition injuries happened on grass and a quarter on turf, but handlers blamed the surface for 42.9% of the turf injuries against about a quarter of the grass ones. The authors caution that a perceived link does not prove cause.
- **Turns load the toes.** The same paper pictures a dog on a running turn on turf with the toes of its inside front leg bent back past straight, notes that front-toe injuries are common in agility dogs, and says turf may need its infill topped up as it ages.
- **Slips happen on every surface.** In a University of Helsinki survey of 864 Finnish competition dogs, slipping was part of 42.3% of the injuries on an obstacle, but surface type was not associated with slipping ([Animals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8833498/)). Two-thirds of the injured dogs (67.6%) were hurt on a surface they used every week, so a familiar yard is no protection on its own.

For a backyard course, the goal is the same footing at every takeoff, turn and landing. Check the infill most where your dog pushes off and turns, and top it up when it thins; see [how often to add infill](/guides/how-often-to-add-infill-to-artificial-turf/) and [infill for dogs](/guides/dog-turf-infill/).

Some turf is sold for exactly this. ForeverLawn's K9Grass Sport, made for canine athletic competition, has a 1 1/2 inch blade and infill the maker calls "optional & adjustable to desired traction level," and the maker says it is designed to reduce the risk of injury ([K9Grass Sport sheet](https://www.foreverlawn.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/K9Grass-Sport-SPEC-M1816_lo-res.pdf)). That is a maker's claim, named as an example: get a sample and try a tight turn on it. Other dog turfs, and the Firestone code section to read before choosing one, are in [pet turf vs regular turf](/guides/pet-turf-vs-regular-turf/).

## Will turf scrape paw pads or overheat a dog in summer?

Pads first. NADAC's standard for its contact obstacles is a fair test for any surface: good grip "without being so rough as to damage the dog’s pads." In the Finnish survey, front paw pads were among the injured spots in 5.9% of the injured dogs; the paper does not say why. Check your dog's pads after a session heavy on turns.

Heat is the bigger risk. A UK study of veterinary records for over 900,000 dogs found exercise was the most common trigger of heat-related illness, 74.2% of cases with a known cause, and that heatstroke from exercise was as likely to kill as heatstroke in a hot car. Young dogs, males and flat-faced breeds had higher odds of it ([Animals, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7459873/)).

Northern Colorado gives plenty of chances. NOAA's 1991 to 2020 normals average 58.3 days a year at or above 90°F at the Greeley UNC station, 48.8 at Longmont 2 ESE and 29.5 at Fort Collins, with June to August highs averaging 88.9°F, 87.5°F and 84.7°F ([NOAA](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-annualseasonal-1991-2020&stations=USC00053553,USC00053005,USC00055116&dataTypes=ANN-TMAX-AVGNDS-GRTH090,JJA-TMAX-NORMAL&format=json&includeStationName=true)). On those days, train early or late, keep sessions short, and keep water and shade by the course. Surface temperatures are in [how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/), and the paw check is in [is artificial turf safe for pets](/guides/is-artificial-turf-safe-for-pets/).

## How do you anchor jumps, tunnels and weave poles without damaging the turf?

Hold things down with weight from above, and let anything a dog can hit fall over.

- **Jumps: anchor nothing.** The AKC requires uprights that can be knocked over and bars that sit loose, and NADAC wants plastic stanchions so that a jump a dog hits "falls easily without causing injury." The Finnish researchers found collisions most common at jumps and said lightweight obstacles soften the impact. Don't stake jump uprights into the turf.
- **Tunnels: bags on top.** The AKC requires a tunnel secured so the dog can't move it, with no holder under it more than an inch and a half thick. NADAC recommends saddle bags holding at least 20 pounds of sand or 3 gallons of water each, 4 bags on a 15-foot tunnel and 5 on a 20-foot one, and bans bungee cords, chains and metal cradles. Bags draped over the tunnel leave no holes in the turf. Don't lock it down harder: the Finnish researchers saw more tunnel injuries than earlier studies and named more fixed attachments among the possible reasons.
- **Weave poles: a weighted base, or holes you plan.** NADAC asks for a base no more than half an inch high, support legs 12 to 18 inches long set off the dog's path, and enough weight to stay put without stakes. A base like that sits on the turf. Stick-in poles are allowed on USDAA's at-home courses, but each pole is a hole. EcoGrass, a turf company, describes the method for any stake: part the blades, cut a small X in the backing between rows, keep sharp edges below the surface, and plug the holes when nothing is in them ([EcoGrass](https://www.ecograssus.com/blog/2020/1/30/inserting-anchor-or-stake-into-turf)). Pick where the weaves will live before the turf goes down.

## Does a jumping dog need a shock pad under the turf?

It is worth asking for. The researchers behind the 2022 survey explain why: a stiffer floor absorbs less energy and sends more force back into the dog's body, while a good floor grips for turns and takeoffs and cushions the landing after a jump. Jumps were the obstacle handlers most often linked to training injuries (37.5%), ahead of contact obstacles (29.7%).

A shock pad is a foam layer between the compacted base and the turf. Under a dog, it has to drain:

- **Pick one that passes water and holds none.** The Motz Group, which sells infill and pads, makes its pads 0.47 to 2.125 inches thick from closed-cell foam designed to let water pass straight through, and says a non-absorbing pad is "essentially required if you have a dog" ([Motz](https://themotzgroup.com/infill_blog/why-use-a-shock-pad/)).
- **It is only as even as the base under it.** Motz also says a pad on a solid base makes the surface uniformly stable in any weather. That is a seller's claim, and the base is the part to check; see [what base goes under artificial turf](/guides/what-base-goes-under-artificial-turf/).
- **The infill question is not settled.** The 2022 survey's authors cite a Finnish finding that more agility injuries happened on rubber-filled turf (35.1%) than on cork-filled turf (12.6%). Those shares sit close to how many of the injured dogs trained mainly on each (32.5% and 12.0%), so they say more about what the dogs ran on than about which fill is safer.

We found no published standard for how much cushioning a jumping dog needs. Ask the installer which pad, how thick, and how it drains.

## What should I ask an installer before building an agility yard?

- **"Where do the seams fall?"** Keep them out of the spots where your dog takes off, turns and lands.
- **"Which turf, what pile, what infill depth, and can I test a sample?"**
- **"Is there a shock pad, how thick is it, and does it absorb anything?"** A good answer names a pad that drains and holds no liquid.
- **"Can the weave poles stand on a base, or where will the holes go?"**
- **"How do I keep the infill even where my dog turns?"**
- **"What does this turf do in July sun?"** Ask for any heat figures the maker publishes.
