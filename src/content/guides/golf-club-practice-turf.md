answer:
  question: "Can a golf course or club use synthetic turf for tees, practice greens or a short-game area in Northern Colorado?"
  answer: "Yes. Colorado law counts a golf course's playing areas, from the driving range and tee boxes to chipping and putting greens, as functional turf. USGA agronomists treat a synthetic tee line as the grass tee's partner for winter, wet and frosty days, and suggest synthetic for target greens and bunker banks. Some town codes add limits, so check yours first."
faq:
  - q: "Could a whole golf course be synthetic under Colorado law?"
    a: "State law doesn't rule it out: its definition of functional artificial turf names the fairway and rough along with tees and greens. Town codes, water rights and what golfers will play on decide the rest, and the USGA articles on this page treat synthetic as a partner to grass on practice features, not a replacement for the course. Not legal advice."
  - q: "Will members actually practice on a synthetic tee line?"
    a: "Some will grumble. A 2008 USGA article says some members object that artificial turf doesn't feel like golf course turf, then answers that neither does bare ground. The same article notes that some courses partly enclose their artificial tees against the weather and for shade, so ask whether a shelter belongs in the plan."
  - q: "Does a synthetic tee line or practice green need irrigation?"
    a: "Not to grow. XGrass lists no water, chemicals or fertilizer among its tee-line features. Rinsing dust off or cooling the surface on a hot afternoon are separate questions, and our guide to watering artificial turf covers both."
  - q: "Should we plow snow off a synthetic tee line?"
    a: "Usually not. SIS Pitches, which builds synthetic sports surfaces, says snow and ice generally should be left to melt and drain on their own, and cleared only when an event needs the surface. Our snow guide covers the tools that won't cut or scrape the turf."
  - q: "Our golf community's HOA owns the practice green. Who decides whether it goes synthetic?"
    a: "The association, inside the town code. Colorado's turf protection for homeowners stops an HOA from banning turf in the backyard of a detached home; it doesn't reach common property. A practice green fits the state's functional-turf definition, and the board's own process and design rules still apply. Not legal advice."

For superintendents, managers, club boards, and the HOAs and metro districts that own golf amenities. Backyard greens are in the [putting greens guides](/guides/putting-greens/); school and park fields have [their own guide](/guides/turf-vs-grass-sports-fields/).

## Where do golf facilities use synthetic turf, and where does grass stay?

Mostly on the practice ground, and mostly beside grass rather than instead of it.

- **The range tee line.** A USGA regional director calls a high-quality artificial hitting surface "a necessity" when the grass tee can't keep up with divots or the range has to open in bad weather, and puts it behind the grass tee so golfers and staff never cross the turf to reach it ([USGA, 2025](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/63/issue-08/getting-the-most-from-your-grass-practice-tee.html)). The same article calls one mat day a week typical and wants 40 to 50 days of recovery for each divoted strip of grass.
- **Target greens, bunker banks and chipping lies.** A USGA agronomist writes that clubs keep asking about synthetic for target greens and practice bunker banks, where presentation and durability matter more than playability. Textures and pile heights now mimic everything from tight fairway to bunker surrounds, so a club can build contoured targets, varied lies around a chipping area and bunker faces that never need regrassing ([USGA, 2025](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/63/issue-13/rethinking-turf--synthetic-options-for-practice-areas.html)).
- **Lessons.** The same agronomist says an engineered synthetic surface is playable soon after a storm, a plus for instruction.
- **Grass stays on the course, and usually on the main range tee.** The USGA says it plainly: "very few golfers enjoy practicing on artificial tees" ([USGA](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/forethegolfer/fake-it-until-you-make-it.html)).

Around here, TPC Colorado in Berthoud lists both a sand-capped tee and an artificial turf tee on its range of more than 350 yards ([TPC Colorado](https://tpc.com/colorado/practice-facility/)). Most practice grounds are grass, and grass needs days off: Highland Meadows in Windsor opens its practice facility every day except Wednesday, for maintenance ([Highland Meadows](https://highlandmeadowsgolfcourse.com/practice-facility/)).

## What does a Northern Colorado winter do to a grass practice tee?

It keeps golfers coming while the grass sits still. Fort Collins averages 150 nights a year at or below 32°F and 51.4 inches of snow (NOAA 1991–2020 normals), yet the city advertises year-round play, "with more than 300 days of sunshine every year" ([City of Fort Collins](https://www.fortcollins.gov/Activities/Golf)). In February 2026, in an unseasonably warm, dry winter, the Loveland Reporter-Herald found golfers hitting range balls at the Olde Course ([Reporter-Herald](https://www.reporterherald.com/2026/02/17/loveland-golf-courses-suspend-carts/)).

The USGA's Central Region director spells out the cost to a grass tee: divots heal far more slowly when it stays open into fall and winter, traffic on dormant turf causes injury that won't recover until growth resumes, and wet weather and frosty mornings are times to close it. Other USGA agronomists call heavy use of artificial turf "common in the shoulder seasons or winter", with a synthetic strip at the back of the tee keeping the range open ([USGA, 2019](https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/course-care/green-section-record/57/5/practice-tees-that-please.html)).

Synthetic has winter limits of its own:

- **Frozen fiber.** SIS Pitches, a synthetic sports-surface builder, says frozen synthetic grass is playable if the footing is adequate, but heavy use should be discouraged because the fiber turns relatively brittle ([SIS Pitches](https://www.sispitches.com/news/winter-pitch-maintenance/)). Ask the maker what its tee line does below freezing.
- **Frozen infill.** The same builder says rain or a quick thaw can flood synthetic turf while the sand infill is still frozen. XGrass describes its own tee line glued to concrete with no infill ([XGrass](https://www.xgrass.com/golf-tee-lines-and-hitting-mats.html)); whatever you're offered, ask how it drains over frozen ground.
- **A base that stays level.** A 2008 USGA article calls for a concrete foundation under mats "so that the teeing surface does not become unlevel" ([USGA, 2008](https://www.usga.org/content/dam/usga/pdf/imported/course-care/080127.pdf)). On clay that the Colorado Geological Survey says can swell up to 20% by volume when wet, that counts for more here; see our [freeze guide](/guides/does-artificial-turf-freeze/).

## How do dry years and water shares change the decision?

Through water shares more than watering stages.

- Fort Collins' water shortage plan restricts only treated water and exempts grass watered from wells or raw water; the City irrigates parks, golf courses and other outdoor lawn areas with 80% raw water ([Fort Collins Utilities](https://web.archive.org/web/20251008135439/https://www.fcgov.com/utilities/img/site_specific/uploads/final-wsap-effective-may-1-2020.pdf)).
- Raw water shrinks in a dry year. Loveland's course superintendent told the Reporter-Herald that in past dry years the city courses received as little as 50% of their water shares, and that greens would be protected first.
- The practice tee competes for that water: the USGA says a grass practice tee needs more water than the course's tees and fairways. The agronomist writing on synthetic practice areas lists water among the inputs that fall, and calls synthetic a dependable choice where native soils are poor or water access is limited.
- Summer cuts both ways. The USGA says northern courses lean on artificial tees in midsummer, when cool-season grass struggles to recover in the heat, but Penn State's sports-surface researchers say synthetic turf generally runs 35 to 55°F hotter than natural grass. Our [heat guide](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/) covers what that means for an unshaded tee line.

## What do Colorado law and Northern Colorado town codes allow on a course?

Statewide, in brief (not legal advice): since 2025, Colorado defines functional artificial turf to include "the playing area of a golf course, such as a driving range, chipping and putting green, tee box, green, fairway, and rough" ([HB25-1113](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_files/40673/download)). Since January 1, 2026, cities, towns and metro districts may not install or allow nonfunctional artificial turf in new development or redevelopment on commercial, institutional or HOA common property, parking lots and medians included ([SB24-005](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_files/45372/download)). A tee line is functional; turf in a parking-lot island is not. Installing turf with intentionally added PFAS has been barred since January 1, 2026 ([SB24-081](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-081)).

Town codes add a layer, mostly where a project goes through a development landscape plan:

## What should a club put in the specification?

For a tee line:

- **Real tees.** The USGA says newer mats let golfers set a tee at the height they want; XGrass says its dense fiber "holds a real tee".
- **Lie length.** The USGA notes longer- and shorter-fibered materials that play like rough or fairway.
- **Swappable wear strips.** The USGA describes track systems that rotate and replace mats, and XGrass sells replaceable hitting-strip stations. The 2008 USGA article warns that long-fiber surfaces, though more realistic, need intensive upkeep and are hard to replace.
- **Placement.** Behind the grass tee, or in front of it with a path, per the USGA's Central Region advice.

For a practice putting or short-game green:

- **Speed.** Ask what stimp the green is built to run and how that compares with the course's greens. Synthetic Grass Warehouse says the type and amount of infill sets a synthetic green's speed; our [stimp guide](/guides/putting-green-stimp-speed/) shows how to measure it.
- **Shot-holding.** Southwest Greens says its sand-filled greens hold a chip from two yards to 200 yards; SYNLawn sells an underlayment it says accepts shots from 160 yards and more. Harmony Club in Timnath describes target areas from 25 to 125 yards at its short-game facility ([Harmony Club](https://harmonyclub.info/golf/)), so ask to see a synthetic green hold shots from the distances your professionals teach. Our [chipping-green guide](/guides/chipping-onto-artificial-putting-green/) explains what stops the ball.

For every feature:

- **Life and payback.** The USGA agronomist puts product life at eight to 12 years and calls up-front costs substantial, though many facilities he has worked with see a return within five to seven years.
- **Golf references.** He advises vendors and installers "who understand the unique demands of golf, not just synthetic turf for landscape or athletic field use." Walk a golf install that is several years old.
- **Paperwork.** A spec sheet and PFAS documentation for every product; our [spec-sheet guide](/guides/turf-spec-sheet-for-colorado/) lists what a sheet should show. Club and common-area work is on the [commercial turf page](/services/commercial-turf/).
