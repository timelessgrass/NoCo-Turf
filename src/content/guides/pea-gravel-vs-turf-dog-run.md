answer:
  question: "Pea gravel or artificial turf for a dog run: which works better?"
  answer: "For a run used every day, turf usually works better: waste sits on top where it can be picked up and rinsed, no stones get tracked inside or shoveled up with snow, and no bare soil turns to mud. Pea gravel drains well, but waste falls between the stones and can't all come out. Both get hot in sun."
faq:
  - q: "Can my HOA stop me from turning a gravel dog run into turf?"
    a: "Not outright in the backyard of a detached home. Colorado law (C.R.S. 38-33.3-106.5) bars an HOA's rules from prohibiting artificial turf there, though the HOA can still apply design guidelines. The run itself is a separate approval in many communities: Barefoot Lakes in Firestone, for one, requires approval for any dog run, keeps it 3 feet from a side lot line and caps it at 200 square feet unless a variance is granted. This is general information, not legal advice."
  - q: "Do Northern Colorado towns limit where a dog run can go?"
    a: "Some do. Wellington's animal code defines a dog run as an area of 144 square feet or less used only to keep dogs, and bars one within 25 feet of another person's home, a public way other than an alley, or a church, school, hospital, public building or municipal park. Whether that reaches a larger dog yard the code doesn't say. It depends on the layout: ask Wellington Planning before you build."
  - q: "Can a dog run beside the house be turf?"
    a: "It depends on where you live. The Severance Shores and Hunters Overlook metro districts in Severance prohibit artificial turf in side yards. Greeley's development code accepts turf only in areas not visible from public streets, so a side-yard run on a corner lot is a question to ask Greeley Planning. A gravel side run avoids those turf rules but not an HOA's dog-run approval."
  - q: "Does Firestone's code say anything about odor in a dog run?"
    a: "Twice. Its turf standard says artificial turf areas may not have odors and calls for a non-toxic, anti-microbial infill such as silica sand or acrylic-coated sand. Its animal code requires owners to remove waste from their own property often enough to prevent offensive odors that disrupt a neighbor, and that one applies to a gravel run too."
  - q: "Can I keep gravel in part of the run and turf the rest?"
    a: "Yes, if the two stay apart. Stones that wander onto turf get kicked down into the fibers and caught by the broom or the snow shovel, so the line between gravel and turf needs a solid edge that holds both in place."

A dog run is a small area that takes a lot of wear: the same path paced along the fence, the same corner used as a toilet, every day of the year. Here is how pea gravel and artificial turf hold up to that. How the run itself is built is on our [pet turf page](/services/pet-turf/).

| In a dog run | Pea gravel | Artificial turf |
|---|---|---|
| What comes inside | Loose stones in paws and coats; mud where the gravel wears through to soil | No bare soil on the surface; loose infill can ride in on paws |
| Poop | Breaks apart and settles between the stones | Sits on top and is picked up whole |
| Urine | Runs straight through into the soil | Drains through the backing into the base; needs rinsing |
| Deep cleaning | Gravel can't be disinfected; the fouled layer gets raked out or replaced | The surface is rinsed and cleaned; the base depends on its drainage |
| Snow | Shovels and snowblowers pick up stones | Can be cleared with care |
| Full sun | Stone stores and radiates heat | Gets hot too |
| A digger | Digs easily and mixes the gravel into the soil | Harder to dig when the edges are fastened |

## Which one keeps dust and mud out of the house?

Turf, mostly because it covers the ground. Pea gravel is a layer of loose, rounded stones, and loose stones move. They lodge between toes and in long coats and ride inside. A dog that paces the fence, digs, or wheels at the same spot pushes them aside until the soil shows through.

In much of Northern Colorado, what shows through is clay. Greeley's water department says local soils have "a layer of hard clay, sometimes just inches below the surface" ([City of Greeley](https://greeleyco.gov/government/city-administration/city-departments/water-and-sewer/save-water/waterwise-landscapes)), and Longmont tells residents its "clay soils absorb water slowly" ([City of Longmont](https://longmontcolorado.gov/water/water-conservation/conserving-water-outdoors/)). Bare clay is dust when it is dry and mud after snowmelt or a spring storm. Raking the stones back over the patch hides it until the next wet week.

Turf keeps one continuous surface over its base, so paws don't reach bare soil. The one thing it can send inside is loose infill, especially right after the turf has been topped up. Which infill suits dogs is its own question: see [which turf infill is right for dogs](/guides/dog-turf-infill/).

## How do you clean poop and urine out of pea gravel compared with turf?

This is the biggest difference, and it favors turf.

In gravel, poop breaks apart as it lands and as the dog walks over it, and the pieces settle between the stones. A scoop gets most of it, not all of it. Urine runs straight through. CSU Extension says pea gravel improves water infiltration into the soil ([CSU Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/mulching/)), which is a plus in a thunderstorm and means urine goes into the soil under the stones. On clay that absorbs water slowly, hosing the gravel pushes it down but not away.

Shelter veterinarians have a standard for exactly this. The Association of Shelter Veterinarians' guidelines call it "impossible to disinfect gravel, dirt, and grass surfaces," and say surface covers such as pea gravel "can be replaced or recovered regularly to reduce contaminant load" ([ASV guidelines, 2022](https://www.aspcapro.org/sites/default/files/2023-05/asvguidelinessecondedition-2022.pdf)). Those rules are written for shelters, but the point carries to a home run: a fouled gravel run gets clean when its top layer is raked out or replaced.

Parasites are why it matters. The Companion Animal Parasite Council says most roundworm eggs need 2 to 4 weeks in the environment to become infective, that common disinfectants don't kill them, and that once they are in the ground only extreme measures get rid of them: paving, removing the topsoil, burning or steam ([CAPC](https://capcvet.org/guidelines/ascarid/)). Its answer is prompt pickup, before the feces break down and scatter. The shelter standard puts it the same way: removing feces daily is acceptable and immediately is preferred. Both are easier on a surface where nothing falls between the stones.

On turf, waste stays on the surface: pick it up, then rinse the spot. How to do both well is in [dog poop on artificial turf](/guides/dog-poop-on-artificial-turf/). Turf is not a sealed floor, though. Urine and rinse water still pass through it into the base and the soil below, which is why the base under a run has to drain.

Where the rinse water ends up matters with either surface:

- Kitchel Lake's rules in Timnath require every dog run to be "free-draining" and say it "shall not discharge urine or diluted waste onto abutting Properties" ([Kitchel Lake rules](https://www.serratogafallsmd1.org/files/6b86004a3/Rules+and+Regs_SFMD1+-+Approved.pdf)).
- Firestone closed two dog parks that sat in detention ponds, citing its state stormwater permit and the bacteria in pet waste ([Town of Firestone](https://www.firestoneco.gov/CivicSend/ViewMessage/message/209671)). Rinse toward soil on your own lot, not toward the gutter.

Town codes also expect a run to be kept clean, whatever it is made of:

- **Greeley:** an animal's containment "must be clean and free of filth, including feces" ([Sec. 10-54](https://library.municode.com/co/greeley/codes/municipal_code?nodeId=PTIICOOR_TIT10AN_CH3VI_S10-54FAPRADSHCO)).
- **Windsor:** the police department's dog brochure says "Private yards and dog runs must be cleaned on a regular basis to prevent odor and build up of feces" ([Windsor brochure](https://www.windsorco.gov/DocumentCenter/View/11963/Dog-Brochure)).
- **Wellington:** the floors of pens are to be cleaned daily, and every place dogs are kept must stay clean and sanitary ([Wellington code](https://library.municode.com/co/wellington/codes/municipal_code?nodeId=CH7HESAAN_ART4AN)).

## What happens to each one in a Northern Colorado snow?

The run gets used on snow days too. The nearest NOAA station to Greeley averages 34.9 inches of snow a year and the Fort Collins station 51.4 inches (1991–2020 normals).

A shovel pushed across pea gravel scoops up stones with the snow, and they end up in the pile, then on the patio or the lawn when it melts. A snowblower is worse: Toro's manual for its Power Clear snowthrower says the machine is not "designed for clearing gravel surfaces" ([Toro manual](https://manuals.toro.com/153265/index.html)). Leave the run unshoveled and a winter's worth of waste sits on and in the snow, then settles into the gravel at the thaw.

Turf can be shoveled or blown. On either surface, pick up waste before the next snow buries it.

## What about heat, digging and a dog that eats rocks?

- **Heat.** Neither surface is cool in full sun. CSU's PlantTalk counts pea gravel among the inorganic mulches that "store and radiate heat" ([PlantTalk 1905](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/topics/water-wise-xeriscape/1905-xeriscape-mulches/)), and the nearest NOAA station to Greeley averages 58.3 days a year at or above 90°F. How hot turf gets, and what cools it, is in [how hot artificial turf gets](/guides/how-hot-does-artificial-turf-get/); rock against turf across a whole yard is in [artificial turf vs xeriscape](/guides/artificial-turf-vs-xeriscape/). Plan shade over the run whichever surface you choose.
- **Digging.** Gravel is easy to dig, and a digger mixes it into the soil underneath. Turf with fastened edges gives a digger less to work with; [can dogs dig through artificial turf](/guides/can-dogs-dig-through-artificial-turf/) covers what stops one.
- **Rock eaters.** VCA Animal Hospitals lists rocks among the things a dog with pica may eat, and says the most common problem pica causes is a gastrointestinal obstruction ([VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/pica-in-dogs)). A dog that eats gravel shouldn't live on it. For dogs that chew, see [is artificial turf safe for pets](/guides/is-artificial-turf-safe-for-pets/).

## Can an existing gravel run be turned into turf?

Usually, and the first question is what happens to the gravel. Before you take a quote, ask:

1. **Does the pea gravel come out, and what goes in instead?** A good answer names the base material, how deep it goes and how it is compacted, and why that suits your soil.
2. **Where does the rinse water go?** A good answer follows it from the turf through the base to a spot on your lot, not onto the neighbor's yard or into the gutter.
3. **How is the turf held down along the fence?** That is where dogs pace and dig. A good answer describes the edge, not just the fasteners.
4. **Which infill, and why for a dog?** See the infill guide linked above.

Town rules can change the plan. In Firestone, turf needs a town permit first, with no fee for a single-family home. The code allows turf on up to 75% of the rear or side yard, but it also requires 50% of the combined side and rear yards to be live plants and says turf can't count toward that, so turf is usually held to about half. It also requires a buffer of at least 3 feet of living, permeable landscape between turf and any property line ([Firestone Development Code 16.6.4](https://library.municode.com/co/firestone/codes/municipal_code?nodeId=TIT16FIDECO_CH6DEDEST_16.6.4LASCFE)). A run along a lot-line fence can't be turf all the way to the fence, and a strip of gravel isn't living or organic material either. It depends on how the Town reads your plan: ask Firestone Planning. This is general information, not legal advice.
